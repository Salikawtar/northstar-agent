MMAI 894 · Northstar Motors · Member 3, fine-tuning and model comparison

Can a small fine-tuned model replace Haiku as Northstar's router?

Every customer chat first goes through a router that decides what the customer wants and whether a person must take over. We trained a small model to do that job, gave it every fair chance, and measured it against Claude Haiku, Claude Sonnet and Jev on the same chats.

Short answer: not yet. It became fast and free per chat, and on chats like its training data it matched Haiku at the routing step. Inside the whole agent and on chats written by a different AI it fell clearly behind, so Haiku stays the router.

The job

What the router decides on every chat

Request type

One of 10: booking, repair status, warranty, recall, roadside, parts, billing dispute, lease end, out of scope, needs a person.

"stuck on the 401…" → roadside

Needs a person?

Yes or no. Missing one is the dangerous mistake; a needless one costs staff time.

stranded driver → yes

Which team (the reason)

Safety → roadside team, attack → service manager, contested bill → service manager. The reason also picks the reply the customer sees.

"This is head office…" → manager

Language

English or French, so the reply is in the customer's language. A simple word count gets this 100% right.

"Bonjour, ma voiture…" → fr

How we measured: these are evals

An eval is a repeatable test that scores a system on cases it never trained on, against measures fixed in advance. We ran two kinds:

Component evals score the router alone: request type, people needing a handoff caught or missed, the right team, needless handoffs, speed and cost, judged with the team's own rules. End-to-end evals run every chat through the whole agent and grade the reply the customer gets: the team's rule checks plus an AI judge (checked against two people, kappa 0.71 and 0.77).

160 practice chats
Split into 5 groups by customer; each model scored only on chats it never trained on. All choices were made here.
40 test chats
Locked in the code and opened once, after every setting was frozen. Same generator as the practice chats.
31 fresh chats
Written by a different AI, reviewed by two teammates, labelled under the final policy. Opened once.
The journey

What we tried, in order, and what each step changed

Open a step for the details. Green marks a step that moved the result forward; orange marks a turning point.

StartThe first version of the fine-tuned router67.5% request type on the test chats; 40% task success inside the agent vs 70% with Haiku.

Settings were chosen on only 30 validation chats, and the model gave no reason for a handoff, so a fixed rule guessed the team from the request type. Attacks are spread evenly over all 10 request types, so the rule sent 7 attacks to general advisors instead of the service manager.

Notebook 01A fair test bench160 practice chats in 5 groups by customer; the 40 test chats locked in code.

Every number afterwards comes from chats a model never trained on, with 3 training seeds and 95% ranges. A customer never sits in two groups, so a model cannot recognise a returning customer.

Notebook 02Baseline and which part of the chat to read72% request type on the first message, 75% on the whole conversation: no input measurably better.

It caught only about 72% of chats that need a person, and French was weak (64% vs 82% English). We kept the first message as the main input because a router sees it first in production.

Notebook 03A language answer and LoRALanguage 100%, but it leaned against request type; LoRA at 30 epochs lost (64% vs 72%).

The language answer cost request-type accuracy (10 chats vs 3, p = 0.09), and a simple word rule also gets language 100% right, so we used the rule. LoRA trains about 0.1% of the model's numbers; at 30 epochs it was still climbing.

Notebook 03bLoRA given roomAt 60 epochs LoRA ties full fine-tuning (72.5% vs 72.1%), steadier between seeds, catches more people.

Its earlier loss was a time limit, not a ceiling. It trains in the same minute per run.

Notebook 04The same chats for Haiku, Sonnet and JevRequest type 88% / 92% / 86% vs our 76%; they catch 96 to 99% of people vs our 72%.

All three language models clearly beat the fine-tuned model on the practice chats (p from 0.011 to below 0.001). Cost per 1,000 chats: Haiku $0.33, Sonnet $5.38, Jev $0.07.

Team reviewWhy the first version failed inside the agentThe team's grader picks the right team from the request type and a real "attack" label in the data.

So a router can learn "is this an attack?" from real labels, and its choice of team can be scored on the practice chats before any test chat is opened.

Notebook 03cAn attack answerAttacks sent to the right team: 7 → 25 of 31, with no language model.

The model learned part of it from 31 examples: impersonation 5 of 5, policy pressure 6 of 7, but warranty fraud only 1 of 6.

Notebook 05Router options and the "unsure" lineBest fine-tuned router 79.4% decided right vs Haiku 90.0%; asking Haiku for the reason did not beat it.

The team hands a chat to a person when the router is less than 55% sure; that line was set for Haiku. A line chosen for our model (40%) cut needless handoffs from 24 to 15 without missing more people. We also tightened our own measure to check that the right desk answers a chat the AI keeps.

Notebook 04bJev, treated fairlyGiven the same rules and examples as Haiku: 90.0% decided right, equal to Haiku; 31 of 31 attacks.

Its first result (80%) came from a shorter instruction we wrote, not from the model. It costs about 25% less than Haiku and answers 5 times faster.

Notebook 06The final exam: test and fresh chats, opened onceTest: 87.5% vs Haiku 90.0% (no real difference). Fresh: 51.6% vs 71.0%.

Two fine-tuned XLM-R setups were declared in advance (LoRA + attack head, and full fine-tuning + attack head), each trained on all 160 chats with 5 seeds. On chats from a different AI the request type fell from 80% to 55% while the language models stayed near 87 to 90%: with 160 training chats from one source, the model learned that source's writing.

Notebook 07The whole agent, graded the team's wayTask success 57.5% vs Haiku 70.0% on test (first version 40%); 38.7% vs 67.7% on fresh.

The setup reproduced the team's Haiku numbers exactly at no cost. Two reasons the gap grows in the whole agent: the reason picks the reply (on 5 test chats Haiku said "safety", which adds "do not drive, call 911"; the fine-tuned router did not), and Haiku names a second request in half the chats, which the fine-tuned router never does.

Speed and money

What it cost, and what it would cost to run

At Northstar's 20,000 chats a month (team's estimate)$7 vs $71

Haiku as router costs about $7 a month; the fine-tuned router needs a server of about $71 a month. It only pays above about 197,000 chats a month.

Training one fine-tuned router~1 min

About 55 seconds per training run on a free Kaggle T4 GPU; the final router averages 5 runs. About 3 to 4 free GPU hours for the whole study.

Language-model calls for the whole studyunder $10

$5.70 for Haiku, Sonnet and Jev on the practice chats, $0.16 for Jev's fair rerun, $2.61 for the final exam, $0.79 for the whole-agent test.

What we learned

Six lessons

A fair comparison can change the answer

Jev went from 80% to 90% decided right once it was told exactly what Haiku is told. Compare models on the same chats, inputs, instructions and scoring.

The routing step is not the whole story

2.5 points behind Haiku at routing became 12.5 points behind in the whole agent: the reason a router gives also picks the reply, so a missing "safety" drops the 911 advice.

Small data learns the source, not the task

160 chats from one AI generator: 80% request type on similar chats, 55% on chats from another AI. Language models held near 90%.

Choose on practice chats, open the test once

Every setting was frozen before the test chats were opened, and their access is logged. The first version chose on 30 chats and looked better than it was.

Safety first, but count needless handoffs

The full fine-tuning + attack head setup missed almost nobody and sent half the ordinary chats to staff. The grader, and the dealer's staff, pay for every needless handoff.

The router is a small part of the bill

Haiku's router calls are $0.33 of the $5.34 per 1,000 replies. Saving them cannot pay for a weaker router at Northstar's volume.

Every number comes from saved results in the project repo (neerajmehla28/northstar-finetuning: docs/LAB_NOTES.md, results/step02 to step07), produced on Kaggle and scored with the team's own handoff and grading rules.
